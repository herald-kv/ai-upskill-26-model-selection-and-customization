Model Selection & Customization

Which model should we use, can we make it ours, and what will it cost?

  • Welcome. Today we stop asking "can an LLM do this?" (Layers 1 to 4) and start asking "which LLM, customised how, and for how much?"
  • No ML background needed. Everything today is about decisions an engineer makes, not maths.
  • Repo: ai-upskill-26-model-selection-and-customization. Ask everyone to have it cloned and setup_check.py passing.
The three questions

Your app works. Now your manager asks…

1 · Which model?

GPT, Claude, Gemini, Llama, DeepSeek… hosted or on our own servers? Does it need to see images?

2 · Can we make it ours?

Our tone, our format, our domain. Prompting, RAG or fine-tuning? What's LoRA?

3 · What will it cost?

Tokens, caching, rate limits, async, batching. Cents in the demo, thousands of dollars a month in production.

The goal: the cheapest, simplest setup that is good enough, and the evidence to show it.

  • These three questions are the three parts of today. Every demo answers one of them.
  • Hammer the thesis: "good enough" is measured on your task, not on a leaderboard.

An analogy

Choosing a model is hiring

The intern

Small models. Fast, very cheap, great at clear, repetitive tasks: sorting, tagging, extracting.

$0.10

per million input tokens

The experienced hire

Mid models. Handles most everyday work: answering from documents, writing, simple tools.

$0.75

per million input tokens

The senior consultant

Frontier models. Hard reasoning, agents, complex code, high stakes. Slower and pricey.

$3 to $5

per million input tokens

You don't hire a senior consultant to sort the mail. Start small, move up only when the task fails.

  • The analogy runs all session. Prices are real (proxy list price, Oct 2026): gpt-4.1-nano $0.10, gpt-5.4-mini $0.75, Sonnet 4.6 $3, GPT-5.5 $5 per million input tokens.
  • That's a 30 to 50× spread on input, and more on output. Model choice is the biggest cost lever there is.
The landscape, October 2026

Two families of models

Closed (proprietary)

You call them through an API. You never see the weights.

  • OpenAI: GPT-5.5, GPT-5.4 mini / nano, GPT-4.1, Codex
  • Anthropic: Claude Opus, Sonnet, Haiku
  • Google: Gemini 3 Pro, Gemini Flash / Flash-Lite

Usually the strongest. Newest features first.

Open-weight

You can download the weights and run them anywhere.

  • Meta: Llama 4 (Scout, Maverick), Llama 3.x
  • Mistral: Mistral Large / Medium / Small, Ministral, Codestral
  • Alibaba: Qwen3, Qwen3-VL, Qwen3-Coder
  • DeepSeek: V3 / V4 · Google: Gemma · OpenAI: gpt-oss

A few months behind the frontier, sometimes less. Full control.

"Open-weight" ≠ "open source": the licence decides what you may do (commercial use, user limits). Read it.

  • Open-weight models are also offered as hosted APIs by many companies (Groq, Together, Fireworks, OpenRouter, and DeepSeek itself). Open doesn't have to mean self-hosted.
  • Licences: Llama has its own community licence with conditions; Qwen, Mistral and gpt-oss are mostly Apache 2.0; DeepSeek uses MIT. Legal should check before production use.
  • Model names change every few months. Teach the families and tiers, not the version numbers.

Part 1 in one slide

How to choose

  • Where can it run? Privacy decides hosted API, your cloud account, or self-hosted.
  • What must it handle? Modality filters the list.
  • How hard is it? Start at the tier the task needs, then try one tier down.
  • How fast, how often? Latency and volume decide the tier, caching and batching.
  • Prove it. 20 to 50 real examples. The cheapest model that passes wins.
  • Stay portable. A gateway (LiteLLM, OpenRouter) and one line of config, so you can switch next quarter.
  • Then the break. Back in 5 minutes for fine-tuning.
Part 2 of 3

Fine-tuning, the concepts

What it changes · when it's worth it · PEFT and LoRA at awareness level

  • About 30 minutes. Concepts only: nobody will train a model today, and you don't need to know how to.
The customisation ladder

Three ways to make a model yours. Climb only when you must.

1
Prompting
Give instructions
Change what you ask: instructions, examples, output format. Minutes to try. Always first.
2
RAG
Hand over the manual
Give the model the right documents at question time. For knowledge, facts, anything that changes.
3
Fine-tuning
Send on a training course
Change the model's weights with your examples. For behaviour: style, format, a narrow skill, at scale.

RAG is for what the model knows. Fine-tuning is for how the model behaves.

  • A new support agent: you give them instructions on day one (prompting). You give them the policy manual to look things up (RAG). After months, you send them on a course so they handle calls the company way without thinking (fine-tuning).
  • A course doesn't help someone remember next week's new policy. That's why fine-tuning is poor at new facts.
What fine-tuning changes

How a model becomes a model

1 · Pre-trainingTrillions of tokens of internet text. Learns language and world knowledge. Months, $10M+
→
2 · Instruction tuning & RLHFLearns to follow instructions and chat helpfully. Done by the provider
→
3 · Your fine-tuneHundreds to thousands of your examples. Hours, $1 to $1,000s

Fine-tuning is good at

  • A consistent tone, style or persona
  • A strict output format, every time
  • Narrow tasks: classifying into your 40 codes
  • Making a small model as good as a big one at one job
  • Shorter prompts (the examples move into the weights)

Fine-tuning is bad at

  • Teaching new or changing facts (use RAG)
  • Fixing a bad prompt you haven't tried improving
  • Working with little or messy data
  • Anything you need next week (it's a project)
  • Explaining where an answer came from (RAG can cite)
  • Steps 1 and 2 are what OpenAI, Anthropic and Meta do. We'd only ever do step 3, and it nudges what's already there.
  • A fine-tuned model can still hallucinate facts; it just sounds more like you while doing it.

Why full fine-tuning is heavy

A 7B model has 7 billion numbers. Training updates all of them.

Full fine-tune · 7B

~112 GB

GPU memory: weights + gradients + optimiser state ≈ 16 bytes per parameter. Several 80 GB GPUs.

Full fine-tune · 70B

~1.1 TB

A cluster. And a full 140 GB copy of the model for every fine-tune you make.

The trick

What if we froze the model and trained only a tiny add-on?

PEFT

Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning

  • Don't dwell on the bytes-per-parameter maths. The point: full fine-tuning needs lots of hardware, and PEFT is the workaround.
LoRA · Low-Rank Adaptation

Don't rewrite the textbook. Add sticky notes.

+ × =
  • The original weights W stay frozen. LoRA learns two thin matrices, B and A.
  • B × A is a full-size change built from very few numbers: 65k instead of 16.8M per matrix.
  • Across a 7B model: ~0.1 to 1% of the parameters trained, often close to full fine-tuning quality for style and format.
  • The rank r (here 8) is the size of the sticky note. Typical: 8 to 64.
  • You never need to implement this. Know: frozen base + small trainable patch = cheap, fast, swappable.
  • Run 03_lora_intuition.py Part A to show a toy 6×6 version with real numbers.

QLoRA

Compress the frozen base to 4 bits, then LoRA on top. A 7B fine-tune fits a gaming GPU or a free Colab T4.

Adapters are swappable

A 7B adapter is ~34 MB vs a 14 GB model. One server, one base model, a different adapter per customer or task (vLLM multi-LoRA).

  • Other PEFT methods exist (adapters, prompt tuning). LoRA and QLoRA are what you'll see in practice.
  • Hosted fine-tuning APIs probably use something like LoRA behind the scenes. You just upload JSONL.
Part 2 in one slide

Before you fine-tune, ask

  • Have I really pushed prompting: clear instructions, examples, structured outputs?
  • Is the problem missing knowledge? Then it's RAG.
  • Do I have hundreds to thousands of high-quality examples, and an eval to prove the fine-tune helped?
  • Will I retrain when the base model is upgraded or retired?
  • Have I checked whether a bigger model with a good prompt, or caching, is simply cheaper?

Overfitting

Memorises the examples, fails on new inputs

Forgetting

Gets better at your task, worse at others

Bad data

Learns every mistake in the examples, permanently

  • In 2026, most teams never need to fine-tune. Prompting + RAG + a well-chosen model covers most cases. Knowing when NOT to fine-tune is the skill.

Token economics

You pay for every token, in and out

cost = input tokens × input price + output tokens × output price
       (prices are per 1 million tokens)

One chatbot request on gpt-5.4-mini, by token count:

system 600
RAG chunks 2,500
history 1,200
out

The user's question is 40 tokens: 1%. The context is 92%. The answer is 7% of the tokens but 33% of the cost, because output costs 6× more.

1 token ≈ 4 characters

≈ ¾ of an English word

Indian languages

Often 1.5 to 3× more tokens for the same meaning

Thinking tokens

Reasoning models bill hidden thinking as output

  • Ask: "Where's most of the money going?" Most people guess the question or the answer. It's the context we send on every call.
  • That's why RAG chunk count, history length and caching are the real levers.

Prompt caching

Pay full price for the same prompt only once

When many requests start with the same long text, the provider keeps the processed start of the prompt and reuses it. Like a kitchen prepping ingredients once for many dishes.

system prompt + handbook (3,600 tokens) ← cached, ~90% off user question ← normal price
ProviderHowCached input priceNotes
OpenAIAutomatic10% (GPT-5.x) to 25% (GPT-4.1)Prompts ≥ 1,024 tokens
AnthropicExplicit cache_control10%; writing costs 125%5-minute or 1-hour lifetime
Google GeminiAutomatic, or explicit caches~10 to 25%Explicit caches charge storage
DeepSeekAutomatic10%On by default
  • It's also faster: the model skips re-reading the cached part, so time to first token drops.
  • Where it pays: long system prompts, few-shot examples, agents (tool definitions are sent on every step), chat (the history keeps the same start), many questions about one big document.
The one rule

Static first, dynamic last

The cache matches the start of the prompt exactly. One changed character early on, and everything after it is a miss.

✗ Breaks the cache every call

Current time: 10:42:17 rules + handbook (3,600 tokens) question

✓ Caches 92% of the prompt

rules + handbook (3,600 tokens) Current time: 10:42:17 · user: Asha · question

Same for user names, retrieved chunks, A/B test flags: anything that changes goes at the end.

  • This is the most common caching bug in real code. Part C of the next demo shows it live: cached = 0.

Rate limits

"429 Too Many Requests": what to do

RPM

Requests per minute

TPM

Tokens per minute: often the one you hit first

RPD / budget

Per day, or a spend cap on your key

Limits are per model and per account or key, and they rise as you spend more. Servers also fail: 500, 503, timeouts.

Retry with exponential backoff + jitter

→

429wait ~1 s

Jitter = a random part of the wait, so 100 clients don't all retry at the same moment. Retry 429/5xx/timeouts; never 400 or 401.

Async & batching

Stop waiting in line

An LLM call is ~99% waiting. While one waits, send the next.

# sequential: 12 × ~1-2 s
for t in tickets:
    classify(t)

# concurrent: ~ the slowest one
await asyncio.gather(*[classify(t) for t in tickets])
# concurrent, but polite
gate = asyncio.Semaphore(4)

async def one(t):
    async with gate:      # max 4 in flight
        return await classify(t)
NeedUseSaving
A user is waitingasync + Semaphore, streamingtime
Many small itemsseveral items in one promptrepeated instructions
Thousands of items, results by tomorrowprovider Batch API~50% of the price
python 03_cost_and_caching/04_async_batching.py
  • Async doesn't change the cost, only the waiting. The Batch API halves the cost but takes up to 24 hours. Use it for evals, nightly reports, back-filling data.
  • Latency on a shared proxy is noisy. If the numbers look odd, rerun.

Takeaways

Five things to remember

  • No best model, only the best model for the job. Test 3 or 4 on your own examples; pick the cheapest that passes.
  • Hosted by default. Self-host for data rules or huge steady volume. Your cloud account is the middle path.
  • Prompt → RAG → fine-tune. RAG for knowledge, fine-tuning for behaviour. Most teams never need to fine-tune.
  • LoRA trains a tiny patch on a frozen model: cheap, fast, swappable. Know it exists.
  • Tokens are money. Context is the cost, caching is the discount, async is the speed-up, batch is half price.
  • Read them slowly. Ask for one-sentence reactions: which one surprised you?
